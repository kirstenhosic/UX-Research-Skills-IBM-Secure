# Concept test

> **Maintainer:** Kirsten Hosic · **Last reviewed:** 2026-08 · **Status:** current
>
> Format: [`../METHODS.md`](../METHODS.md). Shared question rules: `EVALUATION-LOOP.md` §4.6.

**One sentence:** Show people a representation of something that does not exist
yet and find out whether it makes sense to them and speaks to a problem they
actually have.

## What it can tell you

- Whether the concept is *comprehensible* — what they think it does, in their
  words, before you explain it
- Which problem they think it solves, and whether that is a problem they have
- What they assume it will do that it will not, which is where the expectation
  debt gets created
- Which parts of the value proposition land and which are noise
- Relative reactions across two or three concepts, when the order is rotated

## What it cannot tell you

Use this verbatim in the plan's methodology section. This section matters more
here than for any other method in this directory, because concept tests are the
most over-claimed studies a team runs.

- **Whether anyone will use it.** Every response is stated preference, and
  intention predicts behavior weakly — a large induced change in intention
  produces a much smaller change in behavior. Enthusiasm in a concept test is
  not demand.
- **Whether anyone will pay for it**, or how much. Stated willingness to pay is
  reliably inflated.
- **Usability.** There is nothing to use. Task performance on a concept stimulus
  measures the stimulus.
- **Prioritization against things that already exist.** A new idea shown in
  isolation always compares favorably with the messy thing they use today.
- **Anything at all, if the stimulus is words only.** A concept described but not
  shown tests vocabulary comprehension. That may be worth testing — say that is
  what you did.

**Every finding from this method is labeled as stated preference in the readout.**
Not in a limitations paragraph at the end. On the finding.

## Session shape

| Section | Time (60 min) | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 5 min | Rapport and orientation |
| **Unprimed context** | 10–15 min | Their current workflow, expectations, and unmet needs — **before any stimulus**. This is the whole ballgame; see below |
| First exposure | 5 min | Show it. Say nothing. Ask what they think it is and who it is for |
| Guided walkthrough | 15–20 min | Section by section. What they expected, what surprised them, what is missing |
| Comparison, if multiple concepts | 10 min | Rotate order across participants |
| Reaction and close | 5–10 min | What problem this solves for them, if any. Wrap-up |

**The unprimed section is the reason the order is fixed.** Once someone has seen
the concept, their account of their current workflow and unmet needs is
contaminated for the rest of the session and cannot be recovered. Any guide that
shows a stimulus before those questions is blocked at the gate, and correctly so.

## Counts

Rule of thumb. Assumes one to three concepts and a walkthrough with probes.

| Session length | Concepts | Walkthrough sections per concept |
|---|---|---|
| 45 min | 1 | 3–4 |
| 60 min | 1–2 | 4–6 |
| 90 min | 2–3 | 4–6 |

More than three concepts in one session produces fatigue and order effects
larger than the differences you are trying to detect.

## Instrument craft

**Ask what it is before you say what it is.** "Looking at this, what do you think
it does? Who do you think it's for?" That first thirty seconds is the highest-value
data in the session and you get exactly one chance at it per participant.

**Never ask them to rate it.** "How useful is this, 1 to 5?" produces a number
with no interpretation and no story, from someone being polite to the person who
made it. Ask what problem it solves for them and listen for whether they have
that problem.

**Never ask them to design it.** "What would you change?" outsources design to
someone who has seen it for four minutes.

**Separate the concept from the execution, out loud.** Participants critique
visual polish because it is the easiest thing to critique. Say the visuals are
placeholder, and when they comment on styling, redirect to what the thing does.

**Rotate order across participants** when showing more than one concept, and
record which order each participant saw. Without rotation you have measured
position, not preference.

**Handle the enthusiasm gap explicitly.** When someone is positive, ask what they
would have to stop doing to adopt it, and what would have to be true for their
team to approve it. Those two questions convert warmth into something with
constraints attached, and they are where the real answer lives.

## Sample size

5–8 per segment for comprehension problems, which surface fast and repeat.
10–15 per segment if the decision rests on relative preference across concepts,
and even then treat the comparison as directional.

## Analysis note

Code comprehension separately from desirability. They are different findings with
different consequences: a concept nobody understands can be re-explained, and a
concept everyone understands and nobody needs cannot be fixed by design. Teams
routinely merge the two and conclude the wrong thing.

## Common failure

The readout says "participants were excited about X" and the roadmap treats that
as demand. Stated preference, stated in a room with the person who built it,
about a thing that costs the participant nothing. Write the finding so it cannot
be read that way.
