# Diary study

> **Maintainer:** Kirsten Hosic · **Last reviewed:** 2026-08 · **Status:** current
>
> Format: [`../METHODS.md`](../METHODS.md). Shared question rules: `EVALUATION-LOOP.md` §4.6.

**One sentence:** Participants log their own experiences over days or weeks, in
the moment, so you see behavior that no single session could reach.

## What it can tell you

- What actually happens over time, rather than what someone remembers happening
- Frequency and rhythm — when the thing occurs, how often, under what conditions
- The gap between how people describe their workflow and how it runs on a
  Tuesday afternoon during an incident
- Context a lab cannot reproduce: interruptions, colleagues, the second monitor,
  the ticket that arrived mid-task
- Change over time — onboarding, adoption, fatigue, workaround formation

This is the closest an interview-family method gets to observation, and it is the
right answer when a research question needs behavior rather than recollection.

## What it cannot tell you

Use this verbatim in the plan's methodology section.

- **What people don't notice.** They log what they register as an event. The
  friction they have normalized is invisible to them and therefore to you.
- **Anything at high precision.** Entries are self-reported and made under
  whatever conditions the participant was in. Treat timestamps and counts as
  approximate.
- **Whether the logging changed the behavior.** Being asked to notice something
  daily makes people notice it. Assume some reactivity and say so.
- **Deep reasoning.** Entries are short by design. The "why" comes from the exit
  interview, not the log.

## Session shape

A diary study is three phases, not a session.

| Phase | Time | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Kickoff | 20–30 min, or a written brief | Set expectations, walk through the tool, run one practice entry together |
| Logging | 5 days to 4 weeks | Entries on a defined trigger or cadence, with light check-ins |
| Exit interview | 45–60 min | Their own entries as the interview material. This is where the meaning comes from |

**The exit interview is not optional.** A diary study without one produces a pile
of fragments and a researcher guessing at them. Budget it from the start.

## Counts

Rule of thumb. Assumes a busy professional participant and entries of 2–5 minutes.

| Study length | Entries expected | Realistic completion |
|---|---|---|
| 5–7 days | 1 per day, or event-triggered | 70–85% |
| 2 weeks | 1 per day or 3–4 per week | 60–75% |
| 4 weeks | 3 per week, plus a weekly summary | 50–65% |

Plan for the dropout, not the ideal. Over-recruit by about a third. More than two
weeks of daily entries is where attrition becomes the study's biggest risk.

## Instrument craft

**Prompts are not interview questions.** They are written to be answered alone,
in two minutes, on a phone, without a moderator to clarify. That changes
everything about how they are worded.

- **One prompt, one thing.** No probes will arrive to rescue a compound prompt.
- **Anchor to the event, not the calendar.** "Next time you get a finding you
  decide not to act on, tell us about it" beats "how was your day with the tool."
- **Ask for the artifact.** A screenshot, a photo, a screen recording, the actual
  error text. Artifacts are the part of a diary study that survives contact with
  a skeptical stakeholder.
- **Say what "counts."** Participants systematically under-log because they are
  not sure the small thing qualifies. Tell them it does.
- **Keep the set stable.** Changing prompts mid-study means the corpus is not
  comparable across the period you wanted to compare.
- **Include one open prompt** every week: "anything else worth telling us?"

**Trigger design is the real decision.** Event-triggered logging (log when X
happens) produces relevant entries and uneven volume. Time-triggered (log daily
at 4pm) produces even volume and a lot of "nothing happened today." Pick against
the research question, and say which you chose and why.

## Sample size

8–12 per segment is a working range: large enough to survive attrition, small
enough that a person can actually read every entry. Reading every entry is the
point — a diary study you have to sample is a diary study that was too big.

## Logistics

- **Compensation scales with burden**, and this method's burden is high. Pay per
  entry or per milestone rather than one lump at the end; it materially improves
  completion.
- **Tooling.** A dedicated diary platform if you have one. Without one, a shared
  form plus a scheduled reminder works — accept the extra collation cost and
  budget for it.
- **Devices.** Ask what they will have to hand at the moment of logging. A
  desktop-only form gets no entries from anyone who works away from their desk.
- **Check in during week one.** Silent participants in the first three days are
  usually silent for the whole study, and a single message recovers many of them.

## Analysis note

Two passes. Within-participant first — what happened to *this* person over time,
because the trajectory is the thing this method uniquely produces. Then across
participants for patterns. Doing it in the other order flattens the longitudinal
signal into a pile of undated quotes, which discards the reason you ran a diary
study instead of interviews.

## Common failure

Treating the log as the finding. The entries are raw material; the exit interview
and the within-participant pass are where the study actually happens. A readout
built straight from entries reads like a quote wall and gets received like one.
